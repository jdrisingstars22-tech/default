In a secularized society divine oaths are impractical, because “modern societies don’t believe ordeals are iudicia Dei, which ordeals require to work” (iudicia Dei is a term meaning “judgments of God,” referring to the superstition that God miraculously intervenes to condemn the guilty and exonerate the innocent).[^714] A society that no longer believes a god is listening cannot swear its litigants into candor, and if that were all there was to it, the American departure would be no departure, but simply modernity.++}

## 0294 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++On the other hand, despite society’s secularization, accompanied by an exploding industry and economy, American history scholars argue that “most ordinary business and nonbusiness disputes probably avoided the courts,” because “[c]ourts were too slow, too expensive,” and “[a] high price for formal law encourages alternatives to develop.”[^715] Secular informal forums are scarce in the modern era of ADR, but the ones that prosper are not confined to what one might label small-scale “primitive” or indigenous societies.[^716] John Griffiths questions whether informal forums inevitably disappear as states consolidate their monopoly over coercive authority.[^717] Those who assume that the rise of secularized state power leads to a decline in informal forums may underestimate the durability and sophistication of informal ordering in complex modern societies.[^718] If anything, Jerold Auerbach argues that lawyers “seem so likely to dominate the search for alternatives that only their voices will be heard—and, therefore, no alternatives will be discovered or developed,” and that “[a] professional community of lawyers and judges has wrenched mediation and arbitration from local communities that once resisted law as an alien value system.”[^719]++}

## 0295 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Shortly before the 1976 Pound Conference, concrete patterns of social regulation remained well documented in the United States in what Sally Falk Moore termed “semi-autonomous social fields.”[^720] These institutional settings generate internal rules and compliance mechanisms that operate as the immediate forces governing conduct.[^721] They maintain sufficient autonomy from formal legal systems whose reformulation of disputes into legal categories transforms and often narrows their original character.[^722] Before Frank Sander persuaded the legal profession that romanticized visions of efficiency could solve the legal system’s ailments, these informal settings persisted; as Pirie puts it, “even at the heart of the modern United States, trade associations discourage their members from turning to state courts.”[^723] From Wisconsin’s corporate executives to New York’s garment industry, while “[i]n theory, working practices in the industry were governed by contracts,” in practice, “[m]ost people were aware of their legal rights, but no one expected to enforce them.”[^724] Most prominently, in the diamond industry, around 80 percent of rough diamonds entering the United States in the 1980s passed through a club that required internal arbitration under its bylaws, a system that proved effective because the threat of exclusion from the club could be devastating.[^725] The decline of these commercial ADR systems is best explained by state penetration and displacement rather than by any structural inability of ADR to scale.[^726] This took the form of state-enabled capital flight from domestic manufacturing for the garment industry and regulatory diversification, which opened the diamond trade.[^727] ++}

## 0296 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Other societies, moreover, passed through the same secularization without giving up the test. The separation of church and state, now the rule in most modern legal systems, has eliminated divine QCMs like the Mesopotamian oath; yet the spirit of the QCM persists in modern practice where authoritative legal rules are unavailable.[^728] While other societies have adapted to this cultural transition,[^729] a series of historical circumstances unique to American society[^730] means that here the oath “once was the principal source of ethical regulation of lawyers, but it is now an often overlooked part of the labyrinth of laws and standards governing lawyer conduct.”[^731] A few examples will show how the oath, or its spirit, persists elsewhere.++}

## 0297 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++The civil codes of Egypt, Iraq, and other jurisdictions still offer the Ancient Near Eastern decisive oath—the mechanism that allows a plaintiff to shift the burden of proof to a defendant who must swear or accept liability—where it continues to screen claims lacking decisive evidence.[^732] In modern Greece, unlike the ABA’s oath, which evolved to permit any defense deemed “honestly debatable”[^733] regardless of its content, Article 116 of the Greek Code of Civil Procedure states a general duty to tell the truth and to conduct litigation according to “good faith and morality.”[^734] By imposing a positive “duty to tell the truth,” the modern Greek code echoes the ancient extra-statutory norm that viewed the abuse of legal procedure not as zealous advocacy, but as the act of a “common enemy” who threatens the moral fabric of the community.[^735] In modern Rome, the calumny oath, discussed in Section IV, “remain[s] embedded in modern [romano-canonical] procedural codes.”[^736] ++}

## 0298 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++In modern China, the Arbitration Law directly embeds the Confucian ideal of social harmony described in Section IV, where disruption is the “cost” of formal litigation, and it “strongly encourages the practice of mediation during arbitration proceedings” and empowers arbitral tribunals to initiate mediation even without a party’s request.[^737] This practice parallels the traditional authority of a village elder restoring communal harmony through deference, face-saving, and relational obligation.[^738] At the grassroots level, People’s Mediation Committees rooted in Confucian community ordering continue to function as a pre-adjudicative filter, preventing tens of thousands of civil disputes from escalating into criminal matters each year.[^739] And in England, where dispute resolution through arbitration and mediation predates the common law itself, medieval lovedays evolved into formalized commercial arbitration through the procedural mechanism of penal bonds.[^740] Unlike the United States’ placement of ADR forums on the same track as litigation, Parliament’s uninterrupted support for pro-arbitration statutes from 1698 through the present has sustained a statutory policy favoring arbitration.[^741]++}

## 0299 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++The lawyer as such is not to blame either. As the Roman material in Section IV showed, the mere presence of lawyers in dispute resolution is not, by itself, transformative. Advocati participated in compromissum for centuries without displacing its private, party-controlled character; by AD 529, Justinian’s legislation required counsel appearing “before arbiters whether ex compromisso or otherwise” to swear “that they will . . . introduce on behalf of their clients what they consider to be right and true,” and to “retire from the case” on discovering it to be “unprincipled or intrinsically hopeless.”[^742] In thirteenth-century Languedoc, legal professionals appeared as advisers to arbiters, and their influence, Fredric Cheyette suggests, ran toward objective normative judgment; the decisive break came when the monarchy and its agents, “with no ties to the local landed class,” proved “not amenable to the pressures and arguments of old-style arbitration.”[^743] Colonial American arbitration similarly transformed, not because lawyers entered, but because underlying social conditions changed.[^744] The difference was not the lawyer but what the lawyer had sworn. The Roman advocate entered the informal forum bound by an oath to abandon a hopeless case; the American lawyer entered it bound by nothing but Sharswood’s “immovable fidelity” to the case he had already taken. ++}

## 0300 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++The unsworn American bar, by contrast, went to war for the forums. Beginning in the 1920s, bar associations formed “unauthorized practice of law” committees to wage turf battles against accountants, collection agencies, trust companies, and title insurers, with uneven success, but with enough effect that the bar “enjoined or prosecuted competition from lay providers of such services as divorce advice, even in markets that lawyers did not serve.”[^745] Every court alternative that emerged in this climate seemed to fail. The “small claims court” movement, launched in 1913 to provide people experiencing poverty with easy access to informal justice, was co-opted by “doctors, utility managers, and department store heads” who used the new machinery to efficiently collect debts from people of modest income, turning a potential alternative forum into a streamlined enforcement arm of the corporate economy.[^746] This consolidation extended even to the rising administrative state, which, while initially promising a separate track of expert governance, eventually succumbed to the bar, which demanded that these agencies legalize their procedures.[^747] ++}

## 0301 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++In sum, it was the profession’s institutional capacity for capturing disputes, and not the lawyer’s presence, that explains what twentieth-century American lawyers did once they entered the newly flattened dispute timeline of informal forums alongside trial.[^748] Lawyers achieved this capture through organized bar associations capable of coordinated lobbying, professional anxiety about losing prominence, and an adversarial culture that colonized every process it touched.[^749] Earlier informal systems maintained their distinctive character precisely because they operated where formal legal institutions were unavailable and where authority derived from communal rather than professional sources.[^750] The American legal profession’s particular infrastructure, such as the American Bar Association itself, dismantled those conditions.[^751] But infrastructure alone is an insufficient scapegoat for the changing culture of ADR. Section IV’s treatment of fifteenth-century England is a counterexample to the assumption that “[l]aw and arbitration have been regarded as mutually exclusive: the rise of the former is taken to imply the demise of the latter.”[^752] In the shire and other English provinces, arbitration was “an integral part of the informal machinery of dispute settlement which existed alongside the courts of law,” complementing litigation rather than competing with it because it “continued to perform functions to which the courts could not aspire: [settling] feuds, [making] peace and [restoring] harmonious social relations between disputing neighbors.”[^753] Something else changed disputes, and, as the third development showed, it was money. ++}

## 0302 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++None of this was fated. Other societies, as commercial and as secular as ours, kept the test; the American profession let it go, by the three choices Section V described. What a profession has chosen it can choose again. I do not propose to rebuild the forums; that cannot be done. But the test never lived in the forums. It lived in the disputant, who had to know the strength of his claim before he could know where to take it. Once a Deliberative Body drops the question “should this dispute be externalized at all?”, it orients its strategic development toward preparing disputes for external forums, working backward from what those forums expect, and forfeits the only occasion to subject its own assumptions, and the biases embedded in them, to authentically cultivated dissent.[^754] The next Section asks who should put that question again, and how.++}
