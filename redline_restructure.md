## 0093 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Every argument is born in a room. Long before an advocate rises to say “May it please the Court,” a handful of lawyers around a table decide which claim to press, which theory to stake it on, which facts to trust. Rooms come in two kinds. The first is insular, filled with colleagues who share the cause, admire the theory, and regard the other side as not merely wrong but benighted. How pleasant it must be to prepare a case in such a room, hearing one’s own convictions echoed back, a little louder, from every seat at the table! This is known as groupthink. But an argument that no friend has doubted will be doubted for the first time from the bench, at the client’s expense. The second kind of room is adversarial by design. There, the advocate’s own colleagues play judge and opponent, asking every question the other side will ask while there is still time to repair whatever they break. The insular room produces hubris. The adversarial room produces winning arguments.++}

## 0094 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++This introductory section visits one room of each kind. During their two-decade campaign against school segregation, the NAACP’s lawyers rehearsed their Supreme Court arguments before a packed hall at Howard University, with professors sitting as the Justices, and sent their new legal theories through a committee of cognitively diverse lawyers who spoke several interpretive languages and attacked them before any court could. Seven decades later, the ACLU careened into court in United States v. Skrmetti, never testing its claim outside its own ideological echo chamber. Both teams believed in the justice of their clients’ cause. One won Brown v. Board of Education, 9–0. The other lost, 6–3.[^725] I begin with the loss.++}

## 0096 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++The Plaintiffs’ case in United States v. Skrmetti was a predictable loss.[^726] It was not simply a matter of a conservative Court voting against progressive interests.[^727] Skrmetti arose from a decade of unrehearsed legal and cultural change.[^728] The Plaintiffs’ lead advocate, Chase Strangio of the ACLU, met the slow, deliberative process of courts[^729] with the corporate sphere’s culture of bullying opponents into capitulation.[^730] By one account, Strangio “was a big part of that online world that was so incredibly vicious . . . in trying to suppress the speech of people who disagree[d]” on the trans rights movement.[^731] Strangio, who had “a reputation that did not tolerate dissent,” allowed the ACLU’s Skrmetti strategy to become an echo chamber suffused with internal strife.[^732] One ACLU lawyer observed that “a dogmatism descends sometimes” and that “you hesitate before you question a belief that is ascendant among your peer group.”[^733] ++}

## 0097 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++This culture yielded three flaws in the Skrmetti advocacy that an outsider could have exposed had the ACLU welcomed dissent.[^734] First, the ACLU rejected incrementalism.[^735] Second, it relied heavily on unproven science.[^736] Third, its legal theories proved unpersuasive.[^737] The Supreme Court rejected the sex-classification theory and upheld S.B. 1 under rational-basis review, expressly declining to resolve the scientific dispute.[^738] The most obvious reason for all three flaws is groupthink. ## 0119 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Before the roadmap, the argument in brief. Every society examined in this Note made a would-be litigant pay to enter its courts, not in money but in risk: an oath sworn before the gods, a reputation staked before one’s neighbors, a rule that the loser paid. These entry fees, which this Note calls Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs), could be paid in only one way, by testing the strength of the claim before filing it. A claim that passed the test went to court. A claim that failed it, and could be satisfied elsewhere, went to the elders’ council, the Roman compromissum, or the church meeting, forums that stood apart from the courts and took in what the courts should not hear.[^801] A claim that failed it but could be satisfied nowhere else, a constitutional claim, say, was repaired first and filed afterward. The field of dispute resolution remembers this arrangement for what it did to court dockets. That is not the point. The point is what the test did to the advocate: it forced him, before he committed himself, to hear his assumptions attacked by people who did not share them, which is precisely the exercise that modern psychology prescribes against groupthink. (In this Note the team that develops a claim is a “Deliberative Body,”[^802] and carrying the claim out of its own chambers and into the adversarial arena is “Externalization.”[^803]) The United States inherited the old arrangement and dismantled it. It moved the burden of sorting claims from the disputant, before filing, to the courts, after filing, and the experiment failed in both of its purposes. The forums it built collapsed onto the same track as trial, so the dockets filled anyway; that failure the field has noticed. The other it has not: there is no longer any occasion on which a Deliberative Body must test its assumptions before the courtroom tests them for it. That is the loss this Note is about, and I have set the whole of it out in the following figure before taking it up in detail.++}

## 0121 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++This Note argues that throughout history, across cultures and civilizations, advocacy teams looked much more like the NAACP than the ACLU, and that, for reasons unique to the history of American law, we have many more teams composed like the ACLU’s than clients deserve or our legal system can tolerate. Section II explains why capable legal teams fall into this trap. Drawing on psychologist Irving Janis’s work on groupthink, it shows that the more like-minded a team becomes, the more zealous its advocacy seems to its own members. This fallacious thinking starts in law school, where “[t]he ideological imbalance that pervades legal academia not only fosters groupthink, it undermines the ability of law schools to train effective legal advocates.”[^804] And as the ACLU’s experience shows, when “legal professionals only engage with like-minded colleagues, they may fail to anticipate and truly understand opposing arguments, to the detriment of client interests.”[^805]++}

## 0122 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section III turns to the Constitutional Convention of 1787, which demonstrates that deliberate institutional design can prevent insular advocacy. The framers, whose client was the new nation,[^806] spent months testing their own claim—that the nation needed a new Constitution—under rules “devised to free the delegates to float new ideas, change their minds, alter course, and flexibly respond to new arguments, evidence, and proposals without fear of recrimination.”[^807] Benjamin Franklin captured the spirit of those rules in his closing speech: “I have experienced many Instances of being oblig’d, by better Information or fuller Consideration, to change Opinions even on important Subjects, which I once thought right, but found to be otherwise.”[^808]++}

## 0123 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section IV traces the 4,000-year history of pre-filing dispute resolution across Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Roman, Traditional Chinese, Indigenous, and Medieval English societies. Each society attached profound personal consequences to formal litigation—consequences this Note calls Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs)—which compelled would-be litigants to test their claims before filing. For example, a Roman advocate had to swear that “he would not present any case that he considered desperate, groundless, or based on untruths,”[^809] while Confucian teaching held that before bringing a claim to court, “the correct attitude would be that of self-scrutiny.”[^810]++}

## 0124 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section V examines the history of American law and explains why the previous section’s practices are categorically distinct from American advocacy today. There, I argue that three historical developments eroded the practice of pre-filing scrutiny. First, the profession chose the zealous advocate over the objective case assessor, adopting George Sharswood’s view that “the lawyer, who refuses his professional assistance because in his judgment the case is unjust and indefensible, usurps the functions of both judge and jury.”[^811] Second, the legal system’s expanding capacity for disputes let lawyers capture the informal forums that had once absorbed weak claims. Third, hourly billing and the American Rule on attorneys’ fees shielded lawyers and clients alike from the cost of pursuing weak claims.++}

## 0125 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section VI explores, in psychology and practice, why modern legal teams no longer test their claims and how they can start again. “A law firm might press a doomed litigation strategy,” Cass Sunstein and Reid Hastie observe, in part because a young lawyer who warns the firm’s senior members “that their current litigation strategy is way off course” may “risk her own position inside the firm.”[^812] Even when an organization’s top litigators are on the case, and even when they prepare through moots and other traditional practices, they can still produce poor advocacy if everyone around them thinks alike or if they do not deliberately seek out cognitively diverse perspectives. ++}

## 0129 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Contemporary Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) has lost the plot. On the prevailing understanding, a “dispute” is a conflict that a team of advocates (a “Deliberative Body”[^2]) has already conveyed from its internal chambers into the adversarial arena (“Externalized”[^3]). Negotiation, mediation, and arbitration earn the name “alternatives” because each can relieve a party’s normative demands.[^4] Choosing among these parallel forums is now the first step in the dispute-resolution process, and whether Externalization is merited at all is no longer a question. That neglect stems from a sustained separation from historical practice.[^5] The 1976 Pound Conference ratified the divorce, when “Frank Sander delivered his famous remarks [and] the modern dispute resolution movement was born.”[^6] And what were Frank Sander’s famous remarks? That “one might envision by the year 2000 not simply a courthouse but a Dispute Resolution Center, where the grievant would first be channeled through a screening clerk.”[^7] This romanticized vision of bureaucratic triage—sorting already Externalized grievances among forums of mediation, arbitration, fact-finding, and trial—has captivated a generation of practitioners and “is often considered to be the point of origin for contemporary dispute resolution.”[^8] Perhaps the ignorance of modern practitioners is forgivable. Like the prisoners in Plato’s cave, chained to face the wall and mistaking the shadows projected before them for the whole of reality,[^9] Deliberative Bodies are chained to Sander’s vision of selecting among external forums and mistake it for the whole of dispute resolution. The field’s theoretical ambitions have narrowed as it matured.[^10] --}

## 0130 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Step outside the cave, and the world that comes into view is arranged nothing like ours. Throughout much of recorded human history, the conceptual analogs to negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and trial did not occupy the same track.[^11] Each of the societies examined in this Note—Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Ancient Roman, Traditional Chinese, and Medieval English—maintained Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs), institutional features that attached profound personal consequences to the act of formal litigation and so compelled would-be litigants to scrutinize the strength of their claims. When that scrutiny revealed a grievance’s deficiencies, the would-be litigant’s first option was not to relinquish their claim but to seek relief through the “Alternative” system instead—whether that was the elders’ councils of the Ancient Near East, the compromissum of Imperial Rome, or the church meetings of colonial Puritan communities.[^12] Their second option, taken when their grievance could only be remedied by the full relief offered via the formal system (i.e., constitutional rights violations), was to address the weaknesses in their claim through pre-filing scrutiny, and to persist in court once they were confident they could prevail for their clients. Historically, this model succeeded in relieving court dockets (what modern ADR claims as its principal benefit), as QCMs sorted disputants into either the formal (strong claims) or informal (weak claims) forums. But QCMs had another purpose. By compelling individual claimants to test the strength of their claims before externalizing, they imposed the adversarial testing of assumptions that modern social psychology has identified as the chief safeguard against groupthink—the compounding of cognitive biases within a cohesive group whose shared objective of externalizing a claim forecloses any occasion for critical self-examination, causing the team to adopt strategies that would not survive the pre-filing scrutiny QCMs once guaranteed. The historical model put the burden of filtering strong and weak claims on the disputant. The American model puts it on courts and administrations. That experiment failed, and the ADR forums it built collapsed onto the same track as trial. It did not merely leave the formal docket full of baseless claims. By removing pre-filing scrutiny from the disputant’s workflow, it eliminated the only institutional occasion on which a Deliberative Body’s assumptions face the pre-filing testing that prevents weak cases from coming into court. Because my thesis involves several moving parts and defined terms, I have presented it in the following figure, which abstracts the main points, before turning to the history.--}

## 0132 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--A hypothetical will illustrate how my theory of ADR operates in practice. The characters and events are fictional; the historical records behind them are not. The setting is Mesopotamia circa 1800 BCE, in the city of Larsa.[^13] A farmer named Shamash-iddin and his family believed their neighbor Warad-Sin had diverted irrigation water from the shared canal, leaving Shamash-iddin’s barley fields to wither.[^14] Before Shamash-iddin would even consider bringing a formal claim before the assembly[^15]—a process requiring a sworn oath before the gods with the risk of divine punishment for baseless claims—he would first test the strength of his claim by presenting his grievance to the neighborhood council of elders.[^16] Shamash-iddin was confident that someone had intentionally diverted the water because levels remained too low this late into the season. ## 0137 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Three points about QCMs warrant emphasis. First, many societies used the term “oath” for their respective QCM, but the concept reaches past a bare sworn affirmation to a supreme deity to a broader spectrum of secular mechanisms designed to force a potential litigant to internalize the existential costs of advancing a weak claim.[^22] Where the QCM took the form of an oath invoking misfortune, it signaled a disputant was “therefore tak[ing] a costly risk, thereby honestly communicating one’s desire to obtain a given verdict.”[^23] --}

## 0138 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Fear of the divine was the source of the oath’s power in Ancient Near Eastern societies. In Classical Athens the “oath” meant something else: the relentless cultural emphasis on individual character enforced through the courts, whose speeches publicized a litigant’s past misconduct and exposed him to informal social sanction.[^24] As Adriaan Lanni observes, because “a litigant’s conduct over the course of his entire life was deemed relevant to the jurors’ decision,” the legal system acted as a disciplinary mechanism wherein “a litigant who wanted to advance baseless claims would have to find witnesses who were willing to put themselves at serious risk to help his case”—a formidable barrier in a society where a majority of citizens held public office at some point in their lives.[^25] This system “gave litigants incentives to investigate their opponents and uncover any past violations of statutory or extrastatutory norms” because an individual who decided to litigate would be “personally presenting their case (and their character) to the assembled jurors, [and] the experience of having one’s character publicly attacked or losing a vote of hundreds of one’s fellow citizens must have been extremely humbling.”[^26] For example, Phrynion, upon a “realisation that his claims that she was his slave were weak,” “drop[ped] that case in favour of arbitration”—deterred by the formal tier’s binary structure that precluded compromise, oaths that risked branding him a perjurer, and financial penalties designed to “make someone hesitate” before obstructing justice.[^27] The “oath” thus had no connection to the divine in Athens, instead serving as a proxy for total reputational risk because the adjudicative process declined to sever the legal claim from the moral standing of the claimant. ## 0349 [edited] BodyTextFirstIndent

Until the mid-twentieth century, attorneys largely operated under fixed-fee, task-based billing arrangements, effectively acting as insurers who personally absorbed the unpredictable costs of litigation.[^499] The 1938 Federal Rules of Civil Procedure permanently transformed this paradigm by introducing open pretrial discovery, a change that radically increased the time, expense, and unpredictability of litigation.[^500] Because lawyers’ fixed fees failed to keep pace with escalating, adversary-driven discovery demands, litigators experienced a severe decline in real incomes throughout the 1940s and 1950s, falling noticeably behind the surging earnings of other professionals, such as doctors.[^501] Seeking to reverse this economic slide and mitigate the unbearable cost uncertainty created by modern discovery, management experts and the organized bar mounted a vigorous campaign throughout the late 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s to transition the profession to hourly billing.[^502]{++ The English got there first. In 1778, Baker records, the Inner Temple’s “age-old obligation to moot was commuted to a cash payment,” and what remained were “perfunctory ceremonies of no educational significance.”[^703]++} While this shift shielded lawyers from the financial risks of drawn-out litigation by transferring the burden entirely to the client, it reversed the direction of the profession’s moral hazard—replacing the fixed fee’s incentive to shirk with the hourly fee’s incentive to pad—a hazard under which[^503] “ADR [became] just another stop in the ‘litigotiation’ game which provides an opportunity for the manipulation of rules, time, information, and ultimately, money.”[^504] 

## 0354 [edited] BodyTextFirstIndent

Here is the critical difference between American history and the prior section’s survey of Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Ancient Roman, Traditional Chinese, or Medieval English societies. For the latter, dynamics such as fear of the divine, the threat of harm to one’s character, and the risk of deteriorating community relations compelled litigants to test the strength of their claims before using the formal system. In the United States, for the first time in human history, the dispute system actually encouraged unscrutinized filing while offering no separate informal forum to absorb the claims that scrutiny would have filtered out—because of the lawyer’s zealous advocacy role, the profession’s institutional capacity for disputes, and both the hourly billing culture and the American rule on fees.[^520] The sole ‘compulsion’ against filing weak or untested claims was Rule 11, yet even after the 1983 amendments strengthened the rule, the 1993 revisions introduced a safe harbor provision that Justice Scalia correctly predicted would render it ineffective.[^521] Part of this has been due to a reluctance to impose sanctions and the subjectivity required to interpret the rule, yet it is hard to imagine an American system where Rule 11 would ever lead to pre-filing scrutiny.[^522] Because Rule 11 is a post-filing cost and not, as in the other societies examined in this Note, a pre-filing one, disputants Externalize grievances without ever confronting how their own cases fare under pre-filing scrutiny.[^523]{-- --}{++ That is not, I think, a small difference. Accountability, Philip Tetlock found, is “much more effective in preventing than in reversing the biased assimilation process”;[^724] a judgment already formed is defended, not examined. Where the old price is still exacted, the old test survives; and it survives, quite naturally, where the advocate must face the same tribunal again and again.
