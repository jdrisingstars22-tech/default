## 0093 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Every argument is born in a room. Long before an advocate rises to say “May it please the Court,” a handful of lawyers around a table decide which claim to press, which theory to stake it on, which facts to trust. Rooms come in two kinds. The first is insular, filled with colleagues who share the cause, admire the theory, and regard the other side as not merely wrong but benighted. How pleasant it must be to prepare a case in such a room, hearing one’s own convictions echoed back, a little louder, from every seat at the table! This is known as groupthink. But an argument that no friend has doubted will be doubted for the first time from the bench, at the client’s expense. The second kind of room is adversarial by design. There, the advocate’s own colleagues play judge and opponent, asking every question the other side will ask while there is still time to repair whatever they break. The insular room produces hubris. The adversarial room produces winning arguments.++}

## 0094 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++This introductory section visits one room of each kind. Seven decades later, the ACLU careened into court in United States v. Skrmetti, never testing its claim outside its own ideological echo chamber. Both teams believed in the justice of their clients’ cause. One won Brown v. Board of Education, 9–0. The other lost, 6–3.[^702] I begin with the loss.++}

## 0096 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++The Plaintiffs’ case in United States v. Skrmetti was a predictable loss.[^703] It was not simply a matter of a conservative Court voting against progressive interests.[^704] Skrmetti arose from a decade of unrehearsed legal and cultural change.[^705] The Plaintiffs’ lead advocate, Chase Strangio of the ACLU, met the slow, deliberative process of courts[^706] with the corporate sphere’s culture of bullying opponents into capitulation.[^707] By one account, Strangio “was a big part of that online world that was so incredibly vicious . . . in trying to suppress the speech of people who disagree[d]” on the trans rights movement.[^708] Strangio, who had “a reputation that did not tolerate dissent,” allowed the ACLU’s Skrmetti strategy to become an echo chamber suffused with internal strife.[^709] One ACLU lawyer observed that “a dogmatism descends sometimes” and that “you hesitate before you question a belief that is ascendant among your peer group.”[^710] ++}

## 0097 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++This culture yielded three flaws in the Skrmetti advocacy that an outsider could have exposed had the ACLU welcomed dissent.[^711] First, the ACLU rejected incrementalism.[^712] Second, it relied heavily on unproven science.[^713] Third, its legal theories proved unpersuasive.[^714] The Supreme Court rejected the sex-classification theory and upheld S.B. 1 under rational-basis review, expressly declining to resolve the scientific dispute.[^715] The most obvious reason for all three flaws is groupthink. Capable advocates do not so much decide that their claims need no testing as let groupthink decide it for them. Skrmetti is a recent, but by no means the only, example of how groupthink wrecks legal strategy, invites predictable defeat, depletes judicial resources, and undermines clients’ causes.[^716] The Strangio-led trans rights movement “didn’t just lose on some of the key issues . . . but lost an enormous amount of background goodwill.”[^717] In a post-Skrmetti interview, Strangio acknowledged these shortcomings and showed himself a litigator willing to learn—which makes Skrmetti all the more telling as a case study in how pervasive groupthink is.[^718] It is troubling that groupthink led a team like Strangio’s to neglect internal dissent. More troubling still is that our modern legal system, stripped of the mechanisms that once compelled advocates to test their claims, does so little to demand it. ++}

## 0118 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++This Note argues that throughout history, across cultures and civilizations, advocacy teams looked much more like the NAACP than the ACLU, and that, for reasons unique to the history of American law, we have many more teams composed like the ACLU’s than clients deserve or our legal system can tolerate. Section II explains why capable legal teams fall into this trap. Drawing on psychologist Irving Janis’s work on groupthink, it shows that the more like-minded a team becomes, the more zealous its advocacy seems to its own members. This fallacious thinking starts in law school, where “[t]he ideological imbalance that pervades legal academia not only fosters groupthink, it undermines the ability of law schools to train effective legal advocates.”[^768] And as the ACLU’s experience shows, when “legal professionals only engage with like-minded colleagues, they may fail to anticipate and truly understand opposing arguments, to the detriment of client interests.”[^769]++}

## 0119 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section III turns to the Constitutional Convention of 1787, which demonstrates that deliberate institutional design can prevent insular advocacy. The framers, whose client was the new nation,[^770] spent months testing their own claim—that the nation needed a new Constitution—under rules “devised to free the delegates to float new ideas, change their minds, alter course, and flexibly respond to new arguments, evidence, and proposals without fear of recrimination.”[^771] Benjamin Franklin captured the spirit of those rules in his closing speech: “I have experienced many Instances of being oblig’d, by better Information or fuller Consideration, to change Opinions even on important Subjects, which I once thought right, but found to be otherwise.”[^772]++}

## 0120 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section IV traces the 4,000-year history of pre-filing dispute resolution across Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Roman, Traditional Chinese, Indigenous, and Medieval English societies. Each society attached profound personal consequences to formal litigation—consequences this Note calls Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs)—which compelled would-be litigants to test their claims before filing. For example, a Roman advocate had to swear that “he would not present any case that he considered desperate, groundless, or based on untruths,”[^773] while Confucian teaching held that before bringing a claim to court, “the correct attitude would be that of self-scrutiny.”[^774]++}

## 0121 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section V examines the history of American law and explains why the previous section’s practices are categorically distinct from American advocacy today. There, I argue that three historical developments eroded the practice of pre-filing scrutiny. First, the profession chose the zealous advocate over the objective case assessor, adopting George Sharswood’s view that “the lawyer, who refuses his professional assistance because in his judgment the case is unjust and indefensible, usurps the functions of both judge and jury.”[^775] Second, the legal system’s expanding capacity for disputes let lawyers capture the informal forums that had once absorbed weak claims. Third, hourly billing and the American Rule on attorneys’ fees shielded lawyers and clients alike from the cost of pursuing weak claims.++}

## 0122 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section VI explores, in psychology and practice, why modern legal teams no longer test their claims and how they can start again. “A law firm might press a doomed litigation strategy,” Cass Sunstein and Reid Hastie observe, in part because a young lawyer who warns the firm’s senior members “that their current litigation strategy is way off course” may “risk her own position inside the firm.”[^776] Even when an organization’s top litigators are on the case, and even when they prepare through moots and other traditional practices, they can still produce poor advocacy if everyone around them thinks alike or if they do not deliberately seek out cognitively diverse perspectives. ++}

## 0123 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Section VII moves from theory to practice. It draws on interviews I conducted with some of the country’s top Supreme Court advocates, such as Carter Phillips and Michael Huston, who told me how much of that theory translates into their work. It closes with the Note’s final case study: Theodore Olson and David Boies’s challenge to California’s Proposition 8. Mindful of his own conservative reputation, Olson recruited his former Bush v. Gore adversary to ensure that any legal attack would be attacked from ideologically diverse perspectives. As Olson later wrote, “[w]e all too often consult only with people who give us the answer we want to hear or the answer that they think we want to hear.”[^777]++}

## 0124 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++Finally, Section VIII concludes with my thoughts on the future of advocacy. For most of recorded history, personal consequences forced disputants to test their claims before they went to court. Today, almost nothing does, so the discipline must come from advocates themselves. Curiously, despite having every incentive under the norms of zealous advocacy and hourly billing to implement my methods, the ACLUs of the world are much more pervasive than the NAACPs. Those methods, as the Note shows, are a prerequisite for remarkable advocacy. We once tested our claims before filing them. We should do so again. ++}

## 0126 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Contemporary Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) has lost the plot. On the prevailing understanding, a “dispute” is a conflict that a team of advocates (a “Deliberative Body”[^2]) has already conveyed from its internal chambers into the adversarial arena (“Externalized”[^3]). Negotiation, mediation, and arbitration earn the name “alternatives” because each can relieve a party’s normative demands.[^4] Choosing among these parallel forums is now the first step in the dispute-resolution process, and whether Externalization is merited at all is no longer a question. That neglect stems from a sustained separation from historical practice.[^5] The 1976 Pound Conference ratified the divorce, when “Frank Sander delivered his famous remarks [and] the modern dispute resolution movement was born.”[^6] And what were Frank Sander’s famous remarks? That “one might envision by the year 2000 not simply a courthouse but a Dispute Resolution Center, where the grievant would first be channeled through a screening clerk.”[^7] This romanticized vision of bureaucratic triage—sorting already Externalized grievances among forums of mediation, arbitration, fact-finding, and trial—has captivated a generation of practitioners and “is often considered to be the point of origin for contemporary dispute resolution.”[^8] Perhaps the ignorance of modern practitioners is forgivable. Like the prisoners in Plato’s cave, chained to face the wall and mistaking the shadows projected before them for the whole of reality,[^9] Deliberative Bodies are chained to Sander’s vision of selecting among external forums and mistake it for the whole of dispute resolution. The field’s theoretical ambitions have narrowed as it matured.[^10] --}

## 0127 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Step outside the cave, and the world that comes into view is arranged nothing like ours. Throughout much of recorded human history, the conceptual analogs to negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and trial did not occupy the same track.[^11] Each of the societies examined in this Note—Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Ancient Roman, Traditional Chinese, and Medieval English—maintained Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs), institutional features that attached profound personal consequences to the act of formal litigation and so compelled would-be litigants to scrutinize the strength of their claims. When that scrutiny revealed a grievance’s deficiencies, the would-be litigant’s first option was not to relinquish their claim but to seek relief through the “Alternative” system instead—whether that was the elders’ councils of the Ancient Near East, the compromissum of Imperial Rome, or the church meetings of colonial Puritan communities.[^12] Their second option, taken when their grievance could only be remedied by the full relief offered via the formal system (i.e., constitutional rights violations), was to address the weaknesses in their claim through pre-filing scrutiny, and to persist in court once they were confident they could prevail for their clients. Historically, this model succeeded in relieving court dockets (what modern ADR claims as its principal benefit), as QCMs sorted disputants into either the formal (strong claims) or informal (weak claims) forums. But QCMs had another purpose. By compelling individual claimants to test the strength of their claims before externalizing, they imposed the adversarial testing of assumptions that modern social psychology has identified as the chief safeguard against groupthink—the compounding of cognitive biases within a cohesive group whose shared objective of externalizing a claim forecloses any occasion for critical self-examination, causing the team to adopt strategies that would not survive the pre-filing scrutiny QCMs once guaranteed. The historical model put the burden of filtering strong and weak claims on the disputant. The American model puts it on courts and administrations. That experiment failed, and the ADR forums it built collapsed onto the same track as trial. It did not merely leave the formal docket full of baseless claims. By removing pre-filing scrutiny from the disputant’s workflow, it eliminated the only institutional occasion on which a Deliberative Body’s assumptions face the pre-filing testing that prevents weak cases from coming into court. {--A hypothetical will illustrate how my theory of ADR operates in practice. The characters and events are fictional; the historical records behind them are not. The setting is Mesopotamia circa 1800 BCE, in the city of Larsa.[^13] A farmer named Shamash-iddin and his family believed their neighbor Warad-Sin had diverted irrigation water from the shared canal, leaving Shamash-iddin’s barley fields to wither.[^14] Before Shamash-iddin would even consider bringing a formal claim before the assembly[^15]—a process requiring a sworn oath before the gods with the risk of divine punishment for baseless claims—he would first test the strength of his claim by presenting his grievance to the neighborhood council of elders.[^16] Shamash-iddin was confident that someone had intentionally diverted the water because levels remained too low this late into the season. Yet his theory fell apart once presented to the council.[^17] One of these elders owned adjacent fields and benefited from the same canal, another was Warad-Sin’s cousin, and a third had previously disputed a property boundary with Shamash-iddin.[^18] The cousin challenged the timeline, the adjacent landowner noted that all farmers faced low water this season, and the rival asked why, if Warad-Sin was stealing water, his own barley looked equally parched.[^19] Confronted with these challenges, Shamash-iddin conceded that his claim was baseless and never brought it.[^20] --}

## 0130 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Before the QCM (the oath) compelled Shamash-iddin to test the strength of his claim, his Deliberative Body—himself and his household—naturally concurred in his judgment that his neighbor was at fault. ## 0134 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Three points about QCMs warrant emphasis. First, many societies used the term “oath” for their respective QCM, but the concept reaches past a bare sworn affirmation to a supreme deity to a broader spectrum of secular mechanisms designed to force a potential litigant to internalize the existential costs of advancing a weak claim.[^22] Where the QCM took the form of an oath invoking misfortune, it signaled a disputant was “therefore tak[ing] a costly risk, thereby honestly communicating one’s desire to obtain a given verdict.”[^23] --}

## 0135 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Fear of the divine was the source of the oath’s power in Ancient Near Eastern societies. In Classical Athens the “oath” meant something else: the relentless cultural emphasis on individual character enforced through the courts, whose speeches publicized a litigant’s past misconduct and exposed him to informal social sanction.[^24] As Adriaan Lanni observes, because “a litigant’s conduct over the course of his entire life was deemed relevant to the jurors’ decision,” the legal system acted as a disciplinary mechanism wherein “a litigant who wanted to advance baseless claims would have to find witnesses who were willing to put themselves at serious risk to help his case”—a formidable barrier in a society where a majority of citizens held public office at some point in their lives.[^25] This system “gave litigants incentives to investigate their opponents and uncover any past violations of statutory or extrastatutory norms” because an individual who decided to litigate would be “personally presenting their case (and their character) to the assembled jurors, [and] the experience of having one’s character publicly attacked or losing a vote of hundreds of one’s fellow citizens must have been extremely humbling.”[^26] For example, Phrynion, upon a “realisation that his claims that she was his slave were weak,” “drop[ped] that case in favour of arbitration”—deterred by the formal tier’s binary structure that precluded compromise, oaths that risked branding him a perjurer, and financial penalties designed to “make someone hesitate” before obstructing justice.[^27] The “oath” thus had no connection to the divine in Athens, instead serving as a proxy for total reputational risk because the adjudicative process declined to sever the legal claim from the moral standing of the claimant. {--This figure shows a principle that recurs throughout the civilizations surveyed in this Note. Up until the Roman Empire, throughout much of human history, litigants were conceptually “pro se,” so to speak.[^29]Accordingly, the historic oath/QCM should not be confused with a modern juror swearing their testimony is truthful or the ABA’s “oath” (if you can call it that), as these QCMs applied to the individual advancing legal arguments in court.[^30] --}

## 0138 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--Rome professionalized counsel, and the calumny oath came with the profession. It required that an advocate “swear that he would faithfully and wholeheartedly strive to present his client’s case justly and truthfully,” that “he would not present any case that he considered desperate, groundless, or based on untruths,” and that “if approached to act in such a case, he would refuse”—a pre-filing certification requirement that bound the advocate’s professional identity to the merit of the claim.[^31] The Roman code also stipulated counsel must “resign from any case that he discovered to be baseless after he had undertaken it” and serve “in the role of gatekeeper for the court, responsible for making a preliminary assessment of a case’s merits and for deciding whether or not a prospective client had a reasonable cause of action.”[^32] A cost-shifting rule (which exists today) reinforced this oath, whereby “the party who lost a lawsuit became responsible for paying all the direct expenses that the winner incurred,” a mechanism designed, in theory, so that “anyone who brought a foolish lawsuit or defended a hopeless case potentially put himself in financial jeopardy.”[^33]--}

{--In both the United States and England the nineteenth-century bar “shorten[ed] and dilute[d]” the lawyer’s oath until it “eliminate[d] most of its meaning.” It reduced what was once “the lawyer’s first, if not his only, personal formulation of ethical standards” to a form that is “often more symbolic than substantive.”[^34] I must interject here that the dilution of the oath is not merely a curiosity of professional regulation but a revealing symptom of American law’s broader tendency to dismantle, one by one, the very QCMs that had historically compelled individual legal advocates to confront the weaknesses of their own positions before presenting them—a tendency whose cumulative effect, as I shall argue, is to leave modern Deliberative Bodies defenseless against groupthink.[^35]--}

## 0140 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--The third point about QCMs concerns their survival. Although the separation of church and state has influenced most modern legal systems—a development that has impacted divine QCMs like the Ancient Near Eastern Oath—the spirit of historical QCM variations persists in modern practice where authoritative legal rules are unavailable.[^36] Fish do not talk about water.[^37] In a secularized society divine oaths are impractical, because “modern societies don’t believe ordeals are iudicia Dei, which ordeals require to work” (iudicia Dei is a term meaning “judgments of God,” referring to the superstition that God miraculously intervenes to condemn the guilty and exonerate the innocent).[^38] While other societies have adapted to this cultural transition[^39], a series of historical circumstances unique to American society[^40] means that the oath “once was the principal source of ethical regulation of lawyers, but it is now an often overlooked part of the labyrinth of laws and standards governing lawyer conduct.”[^41] What follows are just a few examples of how the concept of the oath persists in modern practice.--}

## 0141 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--The civil codes of Egypt, Iraq, and other jurisdictions still offer the Ancient Near Eastern decisive oath—the mechanism that allows a plaintiff to shift the burden of proof to a defendant who must swear or accept liability—where it continues to screen claims lacking decisive evidence.[^42] In modern Greece, unlike the ABA’s oath, which evolved to permit any defense deemed “honestly debatable”[^43] regardless of its content, Article 116 of the Greek Code of Civil Procedure states a general duty to tell the truth and to conduct litigation according to “good faith and morality.”[^44] By imposing a positive “duty to tell the truth,” the modern Greek code echoes the ancient extra-statutory norm that viewed the abuse of legal procedure not as zealous advocacy, but as the act of a “common enemy” who threatens the moral fabric of the community.[^45] In modern Rome, the calumny oath, discussed above, “remain[s] embedded in modern [romano-canonical] procedural codes.”[^46] --}

## 0143 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--QCMs have served, and in much of the world continue to serve, an indispensable purpose. ## 0153 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent2

{--First, after the nation’s founding, the legal profession stood at an identity crossroads. David Hoffman cast the lawyer as an objective case assessor. ## 0154 [DELETED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{--The profession adopted Sharswood’s view as “the basis for the Canons [which] later evolved into the Model Code and the Model Rules.”[^62] As a result, “one byproduct of an adversary model is that it polarizes the participants, imposing pressures on them to dogmatically pursue their own perceived interests or their own assessments of the proper outcomes of their cases”.[^63] The Model Code and Model Rules carried Sharswood’s principle that “the responsibility for advising as to questionable transactions, for bringing questionable suits, for urging questionable defenses, [was] the lawyer's responsibility.”[^64] Delegating case assessment to the lawyer’s sole province proved particularly improvident in an era in which “private interests were primary” and “[w]hether a lawyer behaved ethically became a test of private conscience, not public honor.”[^65] --}

{--Dan Simon and Minwoo Ahn describe a logic of reciprocity within the adversarial mindset. ## 0254 [INSERTED PARAGRAPH] BodyTextFirstIndent

{++The world that comes into view in the historical record is arranged nothing like ours. Throughout much of recorded human history, the conceptual analogs to negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and trial did not occupy the same track.[^789] Each of the societies examined in this Note—Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Ancient Roman, Traditional Chinese, and Medieval English—maintained Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs), institutional features that attached profound personal consequences to the act of formal litigation and so compelled would-be litigants to scrutinize the strength of their claims. When that scrutiny revealed a grievance’s deficiencies, the would-be litigant’s first option was not to relinquish their claim but to seek relief through the “Alternative” system instead—whether that was the elders’ councils of the Ancient Near East, the compromissum of Imperial Rome, or the church meetings of colonial Puritan communities.[^790] Their second option, taken when their grievance could only be remedied by the full relief offered via the formal system (i.e., constitutional rights violations), was to address the weaknesses in their claim through pre-filing scrutiny, and to persist in court once they were confident they could prevail for their clients. Historically, this model succeeded in relieving court dockets (what modern ADR claims as its principal benefit), as QCMs sorted disputants into either the formal (strong claims) or informal (weak claims) forums. But QCMs had another purpose. By compelling individual claimants to test the strength of their claims before externalizing, they imposed the adversarial testing of assumptions that modern social psychology has identified as the chief safeguard against groupthink—the compounding of cognitive biases within a cohesive group whose shared objective of externalizing a claim forecloses any occasion for critical self-examination, causing the team to adopt strategies that would not survive the pre-filing scrutiny QCMs once guaranteed. The historical model put the burden of filtering strong and weak claims on the disputant. The American model puts it on courts and administrations. That experiment failed, and the ADR forums it built collapsed onto the same track as trial. It did not merely leave the formal docket full of baseless claims. By removing pre-filing scrutiny from the disputant’s workflow, it eliminated the only institutional occasion on which a Deliberative Body’s assumptions face the pre-filing testing that prevents weak cases from coming into court. {++Put simply, history impacts the present, where the accepted institutional practice of enthusiastically bringing as many disputes as possible into the adversarial arena, ostensibly to further both zealous advocacy and billable-hour goals, allows cognitive biases to go unchecked and overlooks the occasion to weed out weak claims. Returning to the Convention, Cass Sunstein argues that the “principal contribution of the framers of the American Constitution lay…in their insistence that diversity is an affirmative good, likely to improve deliberation.”[^591] Their backgrounds were similar, as Section {--IV--}{++III++} noted. But the American colonies were far from a monolithic entity; they evolved into thirteen separate legal systems, shaped by distinct geographies, religious ideologies, and settlement dates, effectively serving as unique political and legal laboratories before 1787.[^592] This created a form of cognitive diversity,[^593] where “the delegates had experiences with twelve very different state political systems, [and] could pool solution proposals with, in some cases, remarkable results.”[^594]
