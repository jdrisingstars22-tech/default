## 9:24 P0095

- Step outside the cave, and the world that comes into view is arranged nothing like ours.
- Throughout much of recorded human history, the conceptual analogs to negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and trial did not occupy the same track.[^11] Each of the societies examined in this Note—Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Ancient Roman, Traditional Chinese, and Medieval English—maintained Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs), institutional features that attached profound personal consequences to the act of formal litigation and so compelled would-be litigants to scrutinize the strength of their claims.  [fn 11]
- When that scrutiny revealed a grievance’s deficiencies, the would-be litigant’s first option was not to relinquish their claim but to seek relief through the “Alternative” system instead—whether that was the elders’ councils of the Ancient Near East, the compromissum of Imperial Rome, or the church meetings of colonial Puritan communities.[^12] Their second option, taken when their grievance could only be remedied by the full relief offered via the formal system (i.e., constitutional rights violations), was to address the weaknesses in their claim through pre-filing scrutiny, and to persist in court once they were confident they could prevail for their clients.  [fn 12]
- Historically, this model succeeded in relieving court dockets (what modern ADR claims as its principal benefit), as QCMs sorted disputants into either the formal (strong claims) or informal (weak claims) forums.
- But QCMs had another purpose.
- By compelling individual claimants to test the strength of their claims before externalizing, they imposed the adversarial testing of assumptions that modern social psychology has identified as the chief safeguard against groupthink—the compounding of cognitive biases within a cohesive group whose shared objective of externalizing a claim forecloses any occasion for critical self-examination, causing the team to adopt strategies that would not survive the pre-filing scrutiny QCMs once guaranteed.
- The historical model put the burden of filtering strong and weak claims on the disputant.
- The American model puts it on courts and administrations.
- That experiment failed, and the ADR forums it built collapsed onto the same track as trial.
- It did not merely leave the formal docket full of baseless claims.
- By removing pre-filing scrutiny from the disputant’s workflow, it eliminated the only institutional occasion on which a Deliberative Body’s assumptions face the pre-filing testing that prevents weak cases from coming into court.

## 9:24 P0111

- QCMs have served, and in much of the world continue to serve, an indispensable purpose.
- They preserve the formal system for genuinely meritorious claims by imposing existential costs on those who would advance a grievance without first having it tested.
- Viewed from a sufficient distance, the same spatial relationship recurs across every civilization surveyed in this Note—two parallel tracks, one formal and one informal, running independently of one another, with QCMs standing between them as the mechanism that determined which path a grievance would travel.
- Modern American ADR has flattened this geometry entirely, compressing both tracks onto a single post-filing plane where negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and litigation sit side by side, distinguished only by procedure and never by whether the underlying claim merited externalization in the first place.[^52] The choice among these forums is merely a choice among post-externalization venues, not a decision about whether to externalize at all.  [fn 52]
- The disappearance of pre-externalization gatekeeping does not merely deprive the system of an administrative filter; it eliminates the institutional occasion on which a claimant’s assumptions were exposed to the kind of heterogeneous, pre-filing scrutiny that groupthink scholarship recognizes as the principal safeguard against the deterioration of group judgment.

## 9:24 P0113

- Why do I turn to history when I could instead focus this Note entirely on the present?
- The short answer is persuasiveness, but the longer answer is that a particular kind of persuasiveness is required.
- The cognitive biases that afflict modern Deliberative Bodies also impacted every Deliberative Body in recorded history, and the societies examined in this Note understood this and built institutions accordingly.
- To convince modern Deliberative Bodies that they should change the manner in which they conceptualize disputes as a process of looking outward, I cannot usefully cite scholarship and empirical studies that conclusively establish the benefits of testing one’s claim before filing it, for these studies, sound as they are, speak only in the idiom of the system that produced the deficiency.
- Instead, my task in writing was to demonstrate that the practice I advocate is not an academic novelty but the recovery of something so embedded in human dispute resolution that its absence, and not its presence, is the historical anomaly requiring explanation.
- In undertaking this approach, I reject the “dominant supposition” that law review notes must merely “prescribe more law” or “address the world in the terms and categories constructed by the official legal apparatus.”[^56] Rather, I submit that groupthink (the cause of poor litigation strategy) can be avoided only if Deliberative Bodies cultivate the ideological diversity required to question those terms before filing.  [fn 56]
- My approach also adheres to current Supreme Court jurisprudence , which asks whether a modern practice is “relevantly similar” to what tradition permits, “apply[ing] faithfully the balance struck by the founding generation to modern circumstances.”[^57] I encourage readers not to dismiss ancient dispute practices as irrelevant to modern legal organizations, for what we know and think about conflict resolution “is not a new fountain gushing fresh from the barren rock of the unknown at the stroke of the rod of our own intellect; it is a stream which flows by us and through us, fed by the far-off rivulets of long ago.”[^58]  [fn 57, 58]

## 9:24 P0117

- Historical formal adjudicative systems had entry fees: divine oaths, reputational exposure, communal pressure, all of which could be ‘paid’ by self-scrutinizing one’s claim.
- In the United States, three historical developments eliminated the fees, thereby extinguishing the ‘payment’ where disputants tested the strength of their claims.[^59] Not only did QCMs disappear, but the informal forums that had historically operated as a coequal track alongside the formal system collapsed into procedural variations of the same adversarial process, causing cognitive biases to run rampant as the shared goal of selecting the optimal forum for a dispute eclipsed any assessment into whether a dispute was worthy of a forum at all.  [fn 59]
- These biases operate on an individual level and across entire legal groups.
- An introduction to each of the three developments follows, along with an explanation of how each contributes to the creation of cognitive bias.
