## P0089

{--Throughout much of recorded human history, before burdening courts with a potentially weak claim, disputants first subjected their claims to the forge of internal dissent. Disputants did this because substantial personal costs were a prerequisite for entering the formal system—costs that served as Quality Control Mechanisms (QCMs) across Ancient Near Eastern, Classical Greek, Ancient Roman, Traditional Chinese, and Medieval English societies. These costs could be ‘paid’ once a disputant tested the strength of their claim, wherein the disputant either (1) entered the formal system with a claim they deemed strong (2) sought relief through a network of independent informal forums or (3) used the scrutiny process to address the case's weaknesses in preparation for the fuller relief offered by the formal system. This process served as an efficiency filter, placing the burden of clearing formal dockets on the disputant while also benefiting the disputant and their client by requiring them to present battle-tested advocacy. The United States inherited the historic dispute resolution tradition and then dismantled the forge of pre-filing scrutiny due to three historical circumstances: the legal profession's choice to cement the identity of the lawyer as a zealous advocate over an objective case assessor, the profession's unique institutional capacity for capturing and colonizing dispute resolution forums, and the widespread transition to an hourly billing model that shielded lawyers from the financial consequences of indulging their own cognitive biases. One consequence of these historical developments is that, because the progressive-era experiment of placing the burden of relieving dockets on the courts failed, litigation, mediation, and arbitration collapsed onto the same track as trial. Thereafter, the informal forum––which historically had taken in weak cases–vanished, thereby depriving the system of an opportunity to take some claims away from formal courts. The greater consequence, however, is that, because there is no 'cost' of entering the formal system with a weak claim, there is no 'payment' for pre-filing scrutiny. It follows that the most important claims—such as constitutional rights violations, which can only be remedied in formal courts—no longer receive the pre-filing scrutiny they once did. At present, the narrow focus on looking outward to external forums rather than inward to the strength of one's case means that legal strategy becomes infected, at both the individual and group levels, with cognitive biases. Left unchecked, these biases compound across a cohesive group (asking now “which external forum is optimal?” and not “should this claim be externalized at all?”) and mature into groupthink — the deterioration of strategic judgment that occurs when a cohesive deliberative body, insulated from adversarial testing of its own assumptions, mistakes internal consensus for external viability. Legal leaders can achieve this by composing cognitively diverse teams, establishing psychologically safe work environments, and mandating structured case discussions. What, then, requires the American advocate to test his claim before he files it? Almost nothing. So, by and large, he does not. The claims that suffer most are the most important ones—constitutional claims that only a court can remedy—and the teams that bring them, when they are insulated from dissent, mistake their own consensus for the strength of their case. For most of human history the untested claim was the anomaly. The setting is Mesopotamia circa 1800 BCE, in the city of Larsa.[^13] A farmer named Shamash-iddin and his family believed their neighbor Warad-Sin had diverted irrigation water from the shared canal, leaving Shamash-iddin’s barley fields to wither.[^14] Before Shamash-iddin would even consider bringing a formal claim before the assembly[^15]—a process requiring a sworn oath before the gods with the risk of divine punishment for baseless claims—he would first test the strength of his claim by presenting his grievance to the neighborhood council of elders.[^16] Shamash-iddin was confident that someone had intentionally diverted the water because levels remained too low this late into the season. We’ve become an alternative to the courtroom.”[^552] Because of the combination of state intervention and lawyers’ importation of litigation tactics into ADR—“litigating” without any of the personal stakes or communal accountability that once preceded Externalization—no reform of the post-filing apparatus can restore what was lost.[^553] The forge must be rebuilt where it can still operate: within the Deliberative Bodies that generate claims and through institutional mechanisms that subject legal theories to the adversarial testing that exposes the biases embedded in them before those theories harden into formal assertions.
