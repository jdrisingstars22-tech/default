Those duplicates are deleted. The first two points are now two short paragraphs that draw back to the survey without repeating it:

> "Three points follow from the survey. The first is the word 'oath.' Most of the societies surveyed used it, or something like it, for their QCM; but as I have described, what mattered was the price and not its form. The Mesopotamian farmer swore a conditional self-curse before gods who were understood to enforce it. The Athenian swore nothing to any god; his 'oath' was his character, staked before hundreds of his fellow citizens who would remember how the vote went. The Confucian litigant paid in the harmony of his community; the Anglo-Saxon accuser paid in witnesses sworn to confirm his charge and in public performance, and the parties at Hagbourne were warned that if the oath were given 'thereafter there would be no friendship.' Each was a device, sacred or secular, for the same end: to set a price on a weak claim, and to collect it before the claim was brought rather than after."

> "The second is who paid. As I have described in discussing Rome, the QCM bound the person who argued the case: the litigant himself, until Rome professionalized counsel, and thereafter the advocate, on whom the calumny oath fell while the loser-pays rule fell on his client. That distinction matters for what follows, because, as I shall describe in Section V, the nineteenth-century American bar diluted the advocate's oath until little of it remained."
