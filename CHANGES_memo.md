## What the edits do

The argument is unchanged. What changes is the order in which its two claims are made, everywhere a reader forms an impression of what the note is about:

1. **First claim, now always first.** For most of recorded history a would-be litigant was compelled to test the strength of his claim before filing (the QCMs), and today almost nothing in the American system compels that testing, so legal teams file untested claims and fall into groupthink.
2. **Second claim, now always second.** The informal forums that once took in the claims testing had found wanting collapsed onto the same track as trial, and "ADR" came to mean choosing among forums after filing.

The title stays. The three historical developments keep their names and order. Section VI's heading is the one structural change proposed; everything else is sentence-level.

## Rule 11

Under the new emphasis, Rule 11(b) is the obvious objection, so the answer is made explicit where the note already gives it. P0306 now says so in terms, brings into the body the Schwarzer sentence your footnote 522 already quotes ("has raised the consciousness of lawyers to the need for a careful prefiling investigation of the facts and inquiry into the law"), and answers it: a raised consciousness of a need is not a compulsion to meet it, and whatever inquiry the rule asks, nothing happens to an advocate for skipping it until after he has filed, and then rarely. The abstract, roadmap, and conclusion are worded to match ("almost nothing" requires testing; Rule 11 "imposes its cost after filing, and rarely").

## Style

New sentences follow Scalia's habits in *A Matter of Interpretation*: short declaratives ("He did not test it out of virtue."), the question answered flatly ("What, then, requires the American advocate to test his claim before he files it? Almost nothing."), "I do not suggest, mind you," "To be sure," "you see," the homely analogy (the toll at the bridge; testing the ice), and antithesis ("It is less that advocates stopped testing their claims because the words changed than that the words changed because advocates stopped testing their claims"). I used the generic "he" for the historical litigant, as Scalia does; the note elsewhere uses singular "their." You may want to settle on one.

Where a sentence of yours was untouched, I left its wording alone even where a Scalia pass would change it ("incentivize" in P0327, "lean into" in P0396, the katana in P0215). Those are yours to decide.
